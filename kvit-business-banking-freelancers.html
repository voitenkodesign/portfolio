Kvit — business banking for Ukrainian freelancers

Kvit is a mobile bank for Ukrainian sole proprietors who get paid by clients abroad. It answers one question: how do you get from sign-up to your first dollar without guessing what the bank wants from you?

Concept. Product, users and data are fictional.

My role

I worked as the only product designer. I framed the problem, cut the scope, designed the flows and screens, and built a small design system with light and dark themes. I also checked the colours for contrast and ran a design critique on the final screens.

The problem

Many Ukrainian developers, designers and marketers work as sole proprietors. They pay 5% tax on income, and their clients pay them in dollars or euros.

The product goal is activation: a first incoming payment soon after sign-up. Three moments get in the way:

  • Verification. A bank can reject a document photo without saying why. People don’t know what to fix, so they give up.
  • Money in transit. A bank can hold a foreign payment until it sees a supporting document. The request often comes by email and doesn’t say which document fits.
  • Income records. Income has to be recorded in hryvnias at the National Bank rate on the day it arrives. Own-card top-ups and refunds end up in the income book and get taxed by mistake.

The common thread: at the moments that matter, people can’t see what the bank wants from them.

The constraints

  • For some holds, the bank can’t tell the customer the exact reason. Every hold message has to be agreed with compliance.
  • The app can’t give tax advice. It can suggest, but the user decides anything that touches tax.
  • Diia, the state ID app, is an outside service. If it’s down, the photo path has to work on its own.
  • The bank can’t see a SWIFT payment before it arrives, so the tracker can’t show earlier steps.
  • A small team: anything that needs new back-office work has to earn its place.
  • All text meets WCAG AA contrast in both light and dark themes.

The process

I started by cutting the scope. My first list had onboarding, verification, two-factor login, invoices, foreign payments, tax reminders and AI tips on spending. That was too much for one story. I picked one path: from sign-up to the first payment received.

I worked from four assumptions, each with a design response:

  • Errors without a reason make people quit. If verification names the cause and the fix, more people will retry.
  • Waiting without knowing is the most stressful moment. If the document request lives inside a payment tracker, holds will clear faster.
  • Tax is simple, records are not. People need help telling income from their own money moving around.
  • Cash flow beats discipline. People want to save for tax, but not lose access to their money.

The roadmap also had AI tips on spending. I dropped them. For this tax group, expenses don’t change the tax. I moved the AI to incoming payments, where a mistake costs money. And instead of a generic money transfer, I designed the one these users make most: selling dollars.

For references, I studied the App Store screens of Mercury, Wise, Qonto, Revolut Business, Copilot and Payoneer. I took principles, not screens: tasks on the home screen, time and fees next to the amount, a review queue for AI suggestions. Payoneer showed me what to avoid: a payment status written as small grey text.

For each key moment, I sketched three options as grey wireframes. I picked one and wrote down what it costs.

Next, I built a small design system: two layers of colour tokens, a type scale, spacing, radii and eight components. I checked every text colour against its background. Two pairs failed at first, so I made them darker. After the visual pass, I checked every pair again, including text on the receipt paper.

Last, I made the final screens and ran a design critique on them. The verdict was honest: the screens were clean but could belong to any fintech app. Cream background, monospaced amounts, the same rounded card everywhere. I did a second visual pass on three key screens, compared them side by side with the first version, and then rolled it out to the whole set.

What I designed

  • Verification that names the error and the fix
  • A tracker for foreign payments, with the document request inside
  • Selling dollars, with a daily limit, a rate hold and confirmation
  • AI flags on income that show their reasons
  • A design system with light and dark themes
  • A loyalty layer with quests and a tax streak

Verification you can fix

Diia comes first. It shares a verified ID, so most people skip manual review. The photo path is a full flow, not a fallback screen.

When a photo fails, the photo itself marks the field that couldn’t be read, and the text says how to fix it. The screen also confirms what was read correctly, so a retry doesn’t feel like starting over. A short panel explains who sees your data and for how long.

While the bank checks the documents, people link the phone, turn on Face ID and set a PIN. Two-factor login fits into the wait.

Money in transit

The home screen has a “Needs your attention” block. Bank requests live there, not in email.

The tracker shows a payment like a parcel: what happened, what’s happening now and what comes next. The key idea: an invoice made in Kvit is already the document the bank wants. The app matches it by client, amount and currency and offers it in one tap.

If a document is rejected, the screen gives the reason, a deadline and a fix. For reasons the bank can’t disclose, it says what happens next and when. When the money arrives, the app converts it at the National Bank rate and adds it to the income book.

My first idea for tax was an automatic pocket that moves 5% of every payment. I chose a one-tap offer on each payment instead: the exact amount, one button and a reminder before the deadline. An automatic transfer can take money right when cash is tight.

Selling dollars is usually the next step, so that’s where the transfer states live: a daily limit with a way around it, a rate that holds for a minute, confirmation and success.

AI flags that show their reasons

The AI never changes the books on its own. It flags incoming payments that may not be income, such as top-ups from your own card or refunds.

A plain “Not income?” suggestion is easy to dismiss without thinking. So each flag shows two or three facts the user can check, and a confidence word instead of a percentage. The wording stays away from tax advice: “may not be income”, never “not taxable”.

Flags wait in one queue instead of interrupting each payment. After an answer, the user can tell Kvit to treat that card the same way from now on. Any answer can be undone until the tax return is filed.

Design system and themes

The name Kvit is short for kvytantsiia, the Ukrainian word for a receipt, so the receipt became the one memorable element. Payment records, statuses and confirmations are printed on warm paper with a torn edge, dotted leaders between a label and its amount, and a document number. On a credited payment, a green stamp shows the date and time. Everything else stays quiet: a cool grey background, navy ink and one cobalt accent.

The type is Golos Text, a typeface designed with Cyrillic in mind. Amounts use it too, large and with tabular figures, so they read as numbers rather than code. A monospaced font is kept only for what a real receipt would print in it: document numbers, rates and dates.

Colour always means one thing. Cobalt is for actions. Green is only for money coming in. Amber means you need to act. Red means something failed. A status is always an icon plus a word, never colour alone.

Components use only semantic tokens, so the dark theme is a second set of values, not a second set of screens. Even the receipt has a dark version.

Loyalty layer

Quests give small cashback rewards. A streak counts quarters with tax paid on time. Three levels raise card cashback.

My first version had a volume quest: send 5 invoices this month. Then I saw the risk. It rewards splitting one invoice into several, which adds noise to the books. In the revised version, quests reward habits that protect the user: paying tax on time, turning on reminders and giving an accountant access. A missed quarter pauses the streak instead of resetting it.

Result

What exists: 20 final screens, three of them in the dark theme, wireframes with three options for each key decision, and a design system with tokens for two themes and eight components.

Success metrics I would track

  • Activation: first incoming payment within 30 days. It’s the goal the whole flow serves.
  • Verification passed on the first attempt. It shows whether errors with a fix actually help people get through.
  • Held payments cleared within 24 hours. It shows whether the tracker and one-tap invoice remove the wait.
  • Support tickets about foreign payments. Fewer tickets mean people understand what the bank wants without asking.

What I learned

  • The strongest idea connected two features that already existed: the invoice and the bank’s document request.
  • In banking, trust is mostly about waiting. People accept checks. They don’t accept not knowing what happens next.
  • When a feature touches someone’s cash, the user should make the move. The product should make it easy.
  • Colour works best when it means one thing. Keeping green only for incoming money made every screen easier to read.
  • Clean is not the same as distinctive. One element taken from the product’s own world did more than polishing every screen.
  • A reward mechanic has side effects. A guardrail metric belongs in the plan from the start.
